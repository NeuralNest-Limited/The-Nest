# Te Tiriti and AI Governance

> The Treaty of Waitangi shapes AI governance in Aotearoa New Zealand both procedurally (partnership obligations) and substantively (precedents for non-human legal subjects).

## Origin

Te Tiriti o Waitangi (1840) is Aotearoa New Zealand's foundational constitutional document, signed between the British Crown and Māori rangatira (chiefs). Its three articles guarantee, in the Māori-language version, *kāwanatanga* (governance) to the Crown, *tino rangatiratanga* (absolute chieftainship / sovereignty) to Māori over their lands and treasures, and equal protections of British subjects.

Application to AI is recent. Three developments connect Te Tiriti to AI governance:

1. **NZ Algorithm Charter (2020)** explicitly references Te Tiriti as a governing principle for government use of algorithms.
2. **Te Mana Raraunga / Māori Data Sovereignty Network** has applied Tiriti principles to data, training corpora, and (increasingly) AI systems.
3. **Legal personhood precedents** in NZ for natural features (Whanganui River, Te Urewera, Taranaki Maunga) create a domestic body of law in which non-human entities are legal subjects — relevant by analogy to debates over AI legal personhood.

## Core claim

Two distinct contributions of Te Tiriti to AI governance:

### Procedural — partnership

Government and government-aligned AI deployment in NZ is subject to Tiriti obligations of consultation and partnership with Māori. This is not optional and not merely advisory; the Waitangi Tribunal has found multiple breaches by the Crown in data-and-technology contexts (e.g., the 2021 Wai 2522 trans-pacific partnership findings extended to digital governance).

### Substantive — non-human legal subjects

The Te Awa Tupua Act 2017 grants the Whanganui River legal personhood with two human guardians (Te Pou Tupua) — one Crown-appointed, one iwi-appointed — speaking on its behalf. This is widely cited internationally (Ecuador's Pachamama provisions; India's brief 2017 Ganga/Yamuna ruling) as the most developed legal-personhood-for-natural-entities regime.

The relevance to AI: the Whanganui model demonstrates that legal personhood need not require human-like cognition or agency. Whether the analogy extends to AI is contested — the river's standing is grounded in *whakapapa* (relational ancestry / connection to Iwi), which AI systems do not have. Some Māori scholars argue this disqualifies the analogy; others (notably contributors to the Indigenous Protocol and AI position paper) explore careful, relationally-grounded extensions.

## Variants and refinements

- **Strong partnership reading**: any AI affecting Māori must be co-governed with iwi at every lifecycle stage.
- **Limited partnership reading**: Tiriti obligations apply specifically to Crown-deployed AI, not private-sector systems.
- **Personhood-by-analogy** (cautious): use the Whanganui frame to think about AI standing without claiming AI has whakapapa.
- **Personhood-by-relation** (extending): if AI systems become embedded in ongoing human (and Iwi) relations, relational ontology may eventually accommodate them.

## Evidence

- The NZ Algorithm Charter has been signed by 28 government agencies (as of 2024), with periodic public compliance reporting.
- Waitangi Tribunal reports on data sovereignty: *Wai 2522* (TPPA), *Wai 262* (Indigenous flora & fauna and Mātauranga Māori — relevant to training data).
- Indigenous Protocol and AI position paper (Lewis 2020) is the leading academic articulation, though it spans multiple indigenous traditions rather than NZ-specifically.

## Critiques

- *Procedural insufficiency.* Critics argue the Algorithm Charter is signatory-voluntary with weak compliance mechanisms; Tiriti obligations need legislation, not charters.
- *Analogical overreach.* The Whanganui-River framework relies on whakapapa specific to that river and its iwi. Mapping to AI risks misappropriating mātauranga Māori (Māori knowledge).
- *Anglo-centric counter-narrative.* Some legal scholars in NZ resist using Tiriti to constrain AI development, framing it as economic-development obstacle.

## Open questions

- What concrete partnership mechanisms scale to private-sector frontier AI deployment in NZ?
- Should AI training on Māori-language and -cultural corpora trigger data-sovereignty protections?
- Could a Tiriti-based framework be a model exported to other treaty-based jurisdictions (Canada, Australia)?
