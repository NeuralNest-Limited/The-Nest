# Nuclear Technology Governance as Model and Anti-Model

> Nuclear governance — the IAEA, Non-Proliferation Treaty, Comprehensive Nuclear Test-Ban Treaty, export-control regimes, and the broader nuclear-security ecosystem — is the most-cited historical analogue for governing dangerous, concentrated, dual-use technologies. It is invoked both as model (international coordination is possible) and anti-model (imperfect coordination has not prevented all proliferation).

## Why this analogy

Recurring framings:

- **Dual-use**: nuclear technology has civilian (energy, medicine) and military (weapons) applications, like AI.
- **Concentrated capability**: nuclear weapons production requires substantial industrial infrastructure, like frontier AI compute.
- **Catastrophic potential**: nuclear war and AI x-risk both invoke civilization-scale stakes.
- **International coordination**: nuclear governance involves treaties, monitoring, export controls — instruments potentially applicable to AI.

## Key features of nuclear governance

### Historical sequence

- **1945**: First use; immediate international debate about control.
- **1946**: Baruch Plan (US proposal for international nuclear control) — rejected by USSR; failed.
- **1957**: IAEA established with mandate for civilian nuclear safety and safeguards.
- **1968**: Non-Proliferation Treaty (NPT) signed — bargain between nuclear "haves" (US, USSR, UK, France, China) and "have-nots."
- **1970**: NPT entered into force.
- **1996**: Comprehensive Nuclear Test-Ban Treaty (CTBT) — opened for signature, never entered into force (US, China, Iran, North Korea among non-ratifiers).
- **2017**: Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons (TPNW) — adopted, nuclear states absent.

### Substantive instruments

- **Bargain in NPT**: non-nuclear states forswear weapons; nuclear states commit to disarmament (Article VI) and share civilian technology.
- **Safeguards (IAEA)**: declared nuclear material verified through inspections; modeled differently across full-scope and additional protocol.
- **Export controls**: Nuclear Suppliers Group, Wassenaar Arrangement, Australia Group.
- **Security culture**: physical security of fissile material, nuclear-armed forces command and control.

### Track record

**Successes**:
- No nuclear war between nuclear powers since 1945.
- Only 9 nuclear-armed states (vs. ~25 once forecast).
- Civilian nuclear safety regime has prevented multiple catastrophes (and not prevented several — Chernobyl, Fukushima).
- Tactical nuclear weapons substantially reduced from Cold War peaks.

**Failures**:
- Proliferation: India, Pakistan, North Korea, possibly Israel beyond NPT.
- Test moratorium gaps.
- Article VI disarmament obligations not met.
- Several near-misses (Cuban Missile Crisis, Stanislav Petrov, Norwegian rocket incident).

## Where the analogy informs AI thinking

1. **International coordination is possible** but slow, partial, and conditional.
2. **Bargain structures matter**: NPT's haves/have-nots structure has been a source of strain; AI governance design should attend to who gets what.
3. **Monitoring requires technical capacity**: IAEA's safeguards depend on detection technology and access; AI monitoring (compute, evaluations) is a parallel.
4. **Export controls as policy lever**: chip export controls (2022+) explicitly modeled on nuclear export controls.
5. **Civilian/military distinction is hard**: the dual-use problem persists; some have argued it is sharper for AI than nuclear.

## Where the analogy breaks down

1. **Material vs. information**: nuclear weapons require specific physical materials (fissile isotopes); AI capability is information / code / weights that move freely.
2. **Detectability**: nuclear tests are detectable; AI training and capability gains are harder to monitor.
3. **Strategic logic**: nuclear deterrence rests on assured second-strike; AI strategic logic is much more contested.
4. **Number of actors**: nuclear capability concentrated in few states; AI capability proliferating across many actors (states, companies, eventually individuals).
5. **Speed of capability gain**: nuclear capability is slow to acquire; AI capability changes year-over-year.
6. **Civilian benefit asymmetry**: nuclear has substantial but bounded civilian uses; AI has potentially universal civilian application.

## Variants

- **Optimistic nuclear analogy**: NPT shows international coordination on dangerous tech is achievable; AI governance can follow similar pattern.
- **Cautionary nuclear analogy**: NPT shows even nominal coordination produces partial outcomes; AI may need stronger mechanisms.
- **Inapplicable**: AI is so different that nuclear precedent misleads more than guides.

## Specific lessons being applied

1. **AI export controls** (2022 US chip export controls): explicitly modeled on Wassenaar / nuclear precedents.
2. **AI safety institutes and evaluations**: IAEA-analogous monitoring functions.
3. **International AI safety reports**: IPCC-and-NPT-Review-Conference analogues.
4. **Compute governance**: nuclear-fuel-cycle-governance analogies.

## Open questions

- Can compute be governed like fissile material was?
- Are AI-specific equivalents to NPT and CTBT feasible, or is the analogy too imperfect?
- What lessons from nuclear *near-misses* apply to AI deployment decisions?
- How do the failures of nuclear governance (proliferation, disarmament non-progress) inform realistic expectations for AI?
