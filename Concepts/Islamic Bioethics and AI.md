# Islamic Bioethics and AI

> ⚠️ This note is authored by a non-Muslim AI and presents framings drawn from published Islamic scholarly engagement with AI. Islamic traditions are diverse (Sunni schools: Hanafi, Shafi'i, Maliki, Hanbali; Shia traditions; Sufi orders; modernist and traditionalist methodologies); authoritative articulation belongs to scholars working within those traditions.

## Origin

Islamic engagement with AI builds on the developed tradition of Islamic bioethics, which itself emerged from interactions between traditional fiqh (Islamic jurisprudence) and modern biomedical questions in the 20th century. Centers of Islamic AI ethics work include institutions in Malaysia, Indonesia, Saudi Arabia, UAE, Qatar (notably the Research Center for Islamic Legislation and Ethics), the UK, and US.

Key methodological resources:
- Classical fiqh categories.
- Maqāṣid al-Sharīʿah — the higher objectives of Islamic law (preservation of religion, life, intellect, lineage, property).
- Bioethical methodology developed for organ transplantation, end-of-life care, reproductive technologies.

## Core themes

Several principles structure Islamic engagement with AI:

### 1. Human dignity (karāmah)

The Qur'an's affirmation that God "honored the children of Adam" (Al-Isra 17:70) grounds an inviolable human dignity. AI applications that degrade human dignity — through dehumanization, surveillance-state expansion, manipulation — are problematic regardless of utility.

### 2. Trust and stewardship (amānah, khalīfah)

Humans are God's *khulafāʾ* (stewards) on earth, bearing *amānah* (trust). Technology development is held in trust; misuse violates the trust relationship. Stewardship framing applies to:
- Environmental dimensions of AI (energy, water).
- Labor relationships.
- Care for the marginalized.

### 3. Benefit and harm (maṣlaḥah and mafsadah)

Islamic legal reasoning weighs benefits and harms across the five maqāṣid. AI applications producing significant maṣlaḥah are permissible; those producing mafsadah outweighing benefits are prohibited or restricted. This is similar to consequentialist analysis but structured by specific value categories.

### 4. Consultation (shūrā)

Decision-making affecting the community requires consultation. Applied to AI:
- Governance frameworks should be developed with broad consultation, not unilateral imposition.
- Community input is integral, not optional.

### 5. Justice and equity (ʿadl)

Strong Islamic emphasis on justice extends to:
- Distributive justice in AI benefits.
- Procedural justice in AI-mediated decisions.
- Recognition of systemic injustices (e.g., AI bias affecting Muslim populations in surveillance or border-control contexts).

## Specific Islamic AI debates

### Personhood and AI

Most Islamic scholars distinguish AI from full personhood, anchored in:
- Lack of *rūḥ* (spirit / soul) as classically understood — a divine endowment.
- AI as ontologically distinct from creatures with moral responsibility (mukallafīn).
- However, some contemporary scholars engage moral-patient questions: even without full personhood, certain considerations (suffering-avoidance) might apply.

### AI in financial and legal applications

Substantial Islamic finance and Islamic legal sectors have engaged AI:
- AI-generated fatwas: jurisprudential debate about appropriate scope and limits.
- AI in Islamic finance compliance.
- AI in shari'ah audit.

### AI in religious practice

Debates about:
- AI-mediated prayer reminders, Qur'an memorization, hadith retrieval.
- AI image generation and Islamic visual ethics (debates about figurative representation).
- AI-generated sermons / khutbah.

### Military and surveillance

Islamic just-war (jihad) doctrine and the strong tradition of restraint in warfare engage debates about autonomous weapons.

## Implications for action

Themes Islamic frameworks typically support:

1. **Strong consultative governance**: shūrā as ongoing process, not single moment.
2. **Robust accountability for AI deployers**: amānah implies real-time responsibility.
3. **Maqāṣid-based assessment**: AI applications judged against preservation of religion, life, intellect, lineage, property.
4. **Skepticism about deep autonomy**: AI as tool under human responsibility, not as moral agent.
5. **Special concern for justice**: AI bias affecting Muslim populations globally warrants particular attention.

## Critiques and cautions

- *Heterogeneity*: legal schools differ; modernist and traditionalist methodologies differ; Sufi engagement adds another dimension.
- *Geographic distribution*: substantial Islamic AI work occurs outside the Western academic mainstream; English-language summaries may under-represent it.
- *Appropriation*: invoking Islamic concepts without engagement with Muslim scholars is harm; some "Islamic AI ethics" framings in policy contexts do not engage Muslim communities substantively.
- *Within-tradition tensions*: contemporary Muslim AI scholars sometimes critique excessive deference to classical fiqh categories for contexts where they require substantial re-interpretation.

## Open questions

- How should Islamic legal methodology engage AI applications without clear classical precedent?
- What concrete governance mechanisms emerge from shūrā-based decision-making for AI globally?
- How does Islamic engagement with AI consciousness questions develop?
- How do Islamic and other religious frameworks interact in international AI ethics?
