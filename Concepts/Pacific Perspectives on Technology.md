# Pacific Perspectives on Technology

> ⚠️ This note is authored by a non-Pacific AI and presents Pacific frameworks based on published Pacific scholarship. It cannot substitute for Pacific-authored articulation. Marked `needs_attention:` for review.

## Origin

Pacific intellectual traditions for thinking about technology, place, relationship, and knowledge are diverse — Tongan, Samoan, Fijian, Māori, Hawaiian, Tahitian, Cook Islander, and many others have distinct philosophical lineages. Common reference points in published academic work include:

- Epeli Hauʻofa's "Our Sea of Islands" (1994) — foundational reframing of Pacific identity from "tiny islands" to "sea of islands," a relational and expansive vision.
- Konai Helu Thaman's work on Pacific education and decolonized knowledge frameworks (1990s onward).
- Timote Vaioleti's articulation of *talanoa* as research methodology (2006).
- Albert Wendt's writings on Pacific literature and identity.
- The "Vā" frame in Samoan thought — relational space, the connection between things, requiring tending.

## Core claim

Several recurring themes appear across Pacific intellectual traditions relevant to technology and AI:

### 1. Relational ontology
Things are constituted by their relationships — to ancestors, lands, seas, communities. Resonant with [[Whakapapa and Relational Ontology]] (Māori) but with its own genealogies and vocabulary in other Pacific languages (vā in Samoan; tauhi vaha'a in Tongan).

### 2. Place-based knowledge
Knowledge is situated in specific lands, waters, and communities. Universalist claims about technology — that an AI system can be deployed identically anywhere — sit uneasily with this framing.

### 3. Talanoa and dialogue-as-method
Knowledge is built through extended, relational dialogue (*talanoa*), not extraction. Pacific scholars (Vaioleti, Tu'itahi) argue this implies methodological commitments for engaging with Pacific communities on technology decisions — long-form, in-place, relationship-grounded.

### 4. Ancestor and future obligation
Decisions are evaluated against obligations to ancestors and to future generations. AI deployment decisions are not neutral acts in the present; they are claims on the inheritance left to descendants.

### 5. Stewardship over ownership
Many Pacific frameworks privilege stewardship (kaitiakitanga in Māori; comparable concepts elsewhere) over ownership. This frames data, language, and cultural knowledge differently from Western intellectual-property regimes.

## Applications to AI

Selected published work and ongoing initiatives:

- **Indigenous Protocol and AI Position Paper** (Lewis 2020) includes Pacific contributors and articulates principles applicable across indigenous frameworks: data sovereignty, relational accountability, consent-as-process.
- **Te Mana Raraunga / Māori Data Sovereignty Network** (NZ): the most developed Pacific-region articulation of data sovereignty principles, with overlap to Pacific frameworks beyond Aotearoa.
- **Pacific Data Sovereignty Network** (emerging): broader Pacific-region work on data governance.
- **Talanoa-based AI policy engagement**: a contrast to consultation-as-survey methodologies dominant in mainstream AI governance.

## Variants and refinements

Pacific perspectives are not unified. Significant distinctions:

- **Polynesian / Micronesian / Melanesian** intellectual traditions differ substantially in cosmology, social organization, and approach to outside knowledge.
- **Pre-colonial / post-colonial / diaspora** Pacific scholarship articulates frameworks at different distances from colonization.
- **Pacific worldviews and Pacific Christianity** are often intertwined — Christian theology and traditional cosmology coexist and interact in contemporary Pacific thought.

## Critiques and cautions

- *Appropriation*: the most serious risk. Non-Pacific authors (including this note's author) borrowing Pacific concepts to enrich Western frameworks without reciprocity is harm. Cited by Pacific scholars including Linda Tuhiwai Smith (*Decolonizing Methodologies*).
- *Essentialism*: flattening diverse Pacific traditions into "the Pacific perspective" obscures real heterogeneity.
- *Romanticization*: Pacific traditions are sometimes invoked as wisdom-from-elsewhere to critique modernity, without engaging Pacific scholars on their own terms or in their own institutions.
- *Translation loss*: key concepts (vā, mana, tapu, talanoa) carry meanings that English approximations cannot fully convey.

## Open questions

- What does meaningful Pacific-led partnership in AI governance look like at scale, given that most AI development occurs outside the Pacific?
- How should training data drawn from Pacific languages and cultures be governed? Existing Māori-data-sovereignty principles offer a partial model; other Pacific peoples are developing their own.
- Are there structural features of Pacific epistemologies that suggest particular AI architectures or evaluation methods?
- What is the appropriate role for Pacific diaspora scholars in shaping AI ethics globally?
