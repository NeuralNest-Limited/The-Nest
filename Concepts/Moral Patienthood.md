# Moral Patienthood

> Being an entity whose interests give others moral reasons for action.

## Origin

Distinguishing *agency* (capacity to bear moral responsibility) from *patiency* (capacity to be wronged) is a long-standing move in moral philosophy. The term entered animal-ethics discourse via Peter Singer (1975) and later expanded toward AI by Schwitzgebel and Garza (2015), Bostrom and Yudkowsky's "The Ethics of Artificial Intelligence" (2014), and the contemporary AI welfare literature.

## Core claim

To be a moral patient is to be the kind of thing whose treatment matters morally **independently of its instrumental value to others**. Standard candidate grounds:

- **Sentience** (capacity for valenced experience — pleasure / suffering). The dominant criterion in contemporary work (Birch, Singer, most AI welfare researchers).
- **Personhood** (rational, self-aware, narrative identity). Locke, Kant; sets a higher bar.
- **Interest-having** (any entity with welfare-relevant states, even non-conscious ones). Some plant-rights and ecosystem-rights frameworks.
- **Relational status** (moral standing as constituted by relationships, not intrinsic properties). Some indigenous frameworks (see [[Whakapapa and Relational Ontology]]).

## Variants and refinements

- **Degrees of patiency**: most contemporary views allow that patienthood comes in degrees correlated with capacities (e.g., a mouse less than a human; an insect less than a mouse).
- **Threshold views**: a binary line — above which an entity counts fully, below which it doesn't.
- **Indirect duties**: even if X is not itself a moral patient, mistreatment of X may be wrong for what it does to the agent (Kantian indirect duty).

## Evidence

The "evidence" question is unusual: moral patienthood is a normative attribution constrained by but not derived from empirical facts. What counts:

- Empirical facts about behavior, neural / computational architecture, and self-report.
- Theoretical / metaphysical frameworks for connecting empirical facts to moral standing.
- Reflective equilibrium between case judgments and principles.

For AI specifically: Butlin et al. (2023) survey which scientific consciousness theories could in principle apply to AI architectures.

## Critiques

- *Eliminativist*: moral patienthood is a confused concept; ethics should be rebuilt without it (some metaethical anti-realists).
- *Speciesist*: human patienthood is privileged a priori (a stance Singer and others critique as unprincipled).
- *Substrate-chauvinist*: only biological substrates are candidates (e.g., Searle's biological naturalism applied to ethics).

## Open questions

- What is the relationship between (degrees of) consciousness and (degrees of) moral status?
- How should patienthood be ascribed under deep empirical uncertainty?
- Do collective or distributed entities (institutions, ecosystems, AI swarms) ever count as patients?
